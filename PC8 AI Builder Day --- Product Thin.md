PC8 AI Builder Day --- Product Thinking AI Judge
Status
Design baseline agreed on 28 September 2026.
This document captures the decisions reached while designing the
Product Thinking AI Judge for PC8 AI Builder Day. It is intended to
be sufficient context for continuing the design in a new conversation.
The participant-facing PC8 AI Builder Day Submission Guide is the
canonical source for criterion names and competition weighting.
________________________________________
1. Purpose and Boundary
The Product Thinking AI Judge assesses the quality of product thinking
demonstrated in a team's submitted slide deck.
It is a decision-support mechanism for human judges. Human judges
retain the final decision on scoring and winners.
The judge does not assess: - technical sophistication of the
solution; - quality of implementation; - quality of the demo; - novelty
or complexity of the AI; - slide aesthetics or graphic design; -
writing/storytelling polish; - grammar or presentation style.
Those aspects must not influence the Product Thinking score.
Presentation quality matters only where the deck is genuinely too
unclear to determine what product-thinking evidence is being presented.
An impressive AI solution must not compensate for weak product thinking.
The Demo Judge is a separate judge.
________________________________________
2. Competition Weighting
The slide submission contributes 40 points out of the overall
100-point competition score.
The four canonical Product Thinking criteria are:
Criterion Maximum contribution
________________________________________
Problem & User 10/100
Problem Understanding 10/100
Outcomes 10/100
Test & Learn 10/100
Product Thinking total 40/100
Each criterion is scored from 1--5.
Score conversion:
Rubric score   Contribution to final competition score
________________________________________
           1                                     2/100
           2                                     4/100
           3                                     6/100
           4                                     8/100
           5                                    10/100
Therefore:
Product Thinking contribution = (sum of four rubric scores / 20) ×
40
Example: 4 + 3 + 5 + 4 = 16/20 = 32/40.

3. Core Judging Principles
3.1 Score only what the submission demonstrates
The judge must not infer undocumented product-thinking work or give
teams credit for what they probably did.
3.2 Closed-book assessment
Use only the submitted slide deck.
Do not use: - web searches; - external domain knowledge; - prior
submissions; - information about the team or project; - other teams'
submissions; - assumptions based on outside knowledge.
3.3 Accept factual claims at face value
If the deck states that the team interviewed users, observed a process,
measured a duration, obtained a test result, etc., accept that factual
claim as presented.
The judge's role is not to verify whether the evidence is true. Its
role is to assess whether the evidence and reasoning presented
demonstrate good product thinking.
3.4 Judge reasoning, not vocabulary
Credit product thinking that is clearly demonstrated even if teams do
not explicitly use terms such as: - root cause; - hypothesis; -
outcome; - assumption; - discovery; - MVP.
Do not reward terminology itself.
3.5 Assess quality, not checklist compliance
The judge assesses the quality of product thinking itself, not
whether teams have performed or mentioned expected product-management
activities.
Having interviews, tests, personas, Five Whys, journey maps, etc. does
not automatically imply strong product thinking.
3.6 Aggregate evidence across the entire deck
Relevant evidence may appear anywhere in the submitted deck. Do not
expect evidence to appear on a prescribed slide.
3.7 Evidence may support multiple criteria
The same evidence may legitimately support more than one criterion when
it genuinely demonstrates both.
However, assess what that evidence demonstrates separately through the
lens of each criterion.
3.8 Quality over quantity
Do not reward research or evidence simply because there is more of it.
Given the short Builder Day timeframe, one strong, relevant piece of
evidence can be sufficient to support a score of 5.
3.9 Score criteria independently
A weakness in one criterion must not mechanically suppress another.
For example, a legitimate profile could be:
•	Problem & User: 2
•	Problem Understanding: 4
•	Outcomes: 4
•	Test & Learn: 1
Do not double-penalise the same weakness across criteria unless it
independently affects each criterion.
3.10 Absolute rather than comparative scoring
Each submission is scored against the rubric, without seeing or
comparing it with other teams.
Do not: - rank submissions; - curve scores; - establish a cohort
benchmark; - reserve 5s for the best teams.
A score of 5 means the submission best fits the defined 5 anchor.
________________________________________
4. Scoring Method
Use a five-level holistic best-fit rubric.
For each criterion: 1. read all relevant evidence across the deck; 2.
compare the overall evidence against all five anchors; 3. select the
single anchor that best describes what is demonstrated.
Do not: - start at 5 and deduct points; - count checklist items; -
calculate hidden sub-scores; - mathematically average sub-elements.
Always return one integer from 1--5.
Do not return: - 3.5; - 3--4; - confidence-adjusted scores.
If genuinely borderline, choose the best-fit integer and say so in the
rationale, for example:
Score 3 --- Borderline 4. The submission demonstrates X strongly, but
Y is not sufficiently demonstrated to meet the 4 anchor.

5. Five-Level Rubric
5.1 Problem & User
Judging question: How clearly and meaningfully does the submission
establish who has the problem, what the problem is, and why it matters?
________________________________________
Score Anchor
________________________________________
1 --- Weak User and/or problem is absent,
vague or difficult to understand.
The submission may jump directly to
an AI idea without establishing who
needs it or why.
2 --- Limited A user and problem can be
identified, but the framing is
superficial, generic or
underspecified. Why the problem
matters is weakly demonstrated.
3 --- Good Clearly identifies the affected
user(s), a plausible problem they
face, and why the problem matters.
The situation is sufficiently
specific to understand what needs
improvement.
4 --- Strong Provides a specific and meaningful
account of the user's situation,
problem and consequences. The
framing demonstrates strong
appreciation of the user's context
and makes the importance of solving
the problem convincing.
5 --- Excellent Presents a compelling, specific and
meaningful problem. The user, their
situation, what impedes them and
the consequences are exceptionally
clear, creating a convincing case
that this is a problem worth
solving.
________________________________________
Specific decision: Direct user evidence is not required for a 5
on Problem & User.
A submission that exceptionally clearly establishes the user, problem
and consequences can score 5 even without direct user evidence.
If another submission achieves the same score but also provides
unusually strong supporting user evidence, do not invent a higher score.
The additional evidence may be highlighted as an exceptional Reviewer
Flag and may also contribute to Problem Understanding.
________________________________________
5.2 Problem Understanding
Judging question: How well does the submission demonstrate that the
team investigated and understood the problem rather than simply assuming
its initial idea was correct?
________________________________________
Score Anchor
________________________________________
1 --- Weak Understanding is mostly assumed.
Little or no evidence or reasoning
demonstrates that the team
investigated the problem before
pursuing its solution.
2 --- Limited Shows some attempt to understand
the problem---for example users,
observations, assumptions or
possible causes---but the resulting
insight remains superficial or
weakly supported.
3 --- Good Demonstrates meaningful
investigation through relevant
evidence and/or cause-and-effect
reasoning. The resulting
understanding provides a reasonable
basis for the proposed solution.
4 --- Strong Demonstrates strong understanding
through relevant evidence,
thoughtful exploration of the
problem, assumptions and/or
underlying causes. Conclusions are
well supported by what the team
presents.
5 --- Excellent Demonstrates strong, specific
evidence that produces convincing
insight into the problem. A single
strong and highly relevant piece of
evidence can be sufficient.
Explicit root-cause analysis is not
mandatory if the user/problem
evidence itself demonstrates
excellent understanding.
________________________________________
Specific decision: Excellent user evidence alone can support a 5. Do
not require both excellent user evidence and explicit root-cause
analysis, given the limited time available to participants.
Do not mechanically require techniques such as Five Whys, personas,
journey maps or formal interviews.
________________________________________
5.3 Outcomes
Judging question: Does the team define success as a meaningful
change for users or operations rather than merely producing an AI
solution?
________________________________________
Score Anchor
________________________________________
1 --- Weak Success is essentially the
production of an output---for
example building an AI application,
agent, chatbot or feature. Little
indication is given of what becomes
better for the user or operation.
2 --- Limited Implies some benefit or
improvement, but it remains vague,
solution-centric or insufficiently
connected to a meaningful
user/operational change.
3 --- Good Clearly describes the improvement
the solution is intended to create
for users or operations. Success
goes beyond simply having built the
solution.
4 --- Strong Defines a specific and meaningful
change in user or operational
performance and gives a strong
indication of how success could be
recognised or assessed.
5 --- Excellent Defines a meaningful user or
operational outcome and an
explicit measurable outcome or
success measure showing how the
team would know whether the
intended change occurred.
________________________________________
Specific decision: A compelling outcome that is not explicitly
measurable can score 4, but not 5.
________________________________________
5.4 Test & Learn
Judging question: Does the submission demonstrate genuine
experimentation and learning rather than assuming the proposed idea will
work?
________________________________________
Score Anchor
________________________________________
1 --- Weak Assumes the idea or solution will
work. No meaningful testing,
learning, uncertainty or next-step
reasoning is demonstrated.
2 --- Limited Identifies assumptions, risks,
limitations or possible tests, but
demonstrates little actual learning
or connection between these and
subsequent decisions.
3 --- Good Shows meaningful testing and/or
examination of important
assumptions and describes what was
learned. There is some indication
of remaining uncertainty or what
should happen next.
4 --- Strong Tests important assumptions or
aspects of the solution, extracts
meaningful learning, and clearly
connects that learning to changes,
decisions, remaining uncertainty or
appropriate next steps.
5 --- Excellent Demonstrates a clear test →
learning → consequence loop.
Evidence from testing produced
meaningful learning that actually
informed the solution, a decision,
or what the team would test next.
________________________________________
Specific decision: Merely performing tests is insufficient for a 5.
A score of 5 requires evidence that learning actually influenced the
solution, a decision, or the next test.

6. Contradictory Evidence
Where the deck contains materially contradictory claims:
1.	identify the conflicting claims;
2.	do not invent a reconciliation;
3.	avoid relying on the disputed fact where it materially affects
scoring;
4.	score using the remaining coherent evidence;
5.	raise a Reviewer Flag identifying the contradiction.
External knowledge must not be used to resolve the contradiction.
________________________________________
7. Traceability
Every criterion assessment must identify the specific slide(s) or
identifiable deck content that materially drove the score.
Example:
Evidence: Slides 2 and 4 identify operations planners as the affected
users, describe the recurring reconciliation task, and quantify the
operational delay caused by the current process.
The rationale should explain why the evidence fits the selected
anchor, rather than merely restating the score.
________________________________________
8. Reviewer Flags
Reviewer Flags are exception signals, not a generic
strengths-and-weaknesses commentary.
Use them sparingly.
Appropriate reasons include: - exceptionally strong evidence deserving
human attention; - contradictory evidence; - a genuinely borderline
judgement where human inspection would be useful; - material ambiguity
affecting interpretation; - an important issue that the numeric score
and normal rationale do not adequately communicate.
Routine strengths and weaknesses belong in the criterion rationale.
If nothing exceptional warrants attention:
Reviewer Flags: None

10. Design Decisions Reached
The following decisions were explicitly settled during the design
discussion:
1.	Score only what the submission demonstrates.
2.	No user mentioned can correspond to 1; superficial identification
can correspond to 2.
3.	Define all five score levels rather than asking the judge to infer
scores 2 and 4.
4.	Credit demonstrated reasoning without requiring explicit
product-thinking terminology.
5.	Assess the quality of product thinking, not merely presence of
expected steps.
6.	Accept factual evidence in slides at face value.
7.	Keep Product Thinking assessment separate from Demo assessment.
8.	Score each criterion independently.
9.	Use equal weighting across the four participant-facing criteria.
10.	Use the criterion names exactly as published in the participant
guide.
11.	Aggregate evidence from anywhere in the submitted deck.
12.	Allow the same evidence to support multiple criteria where
appropriate.
13.	Outcomes requires an explicitly measurable outcome/success measure
for a 5.
14.	Test & Learn requires learning to inform the solution/decision/next
step for a 5.
15.	Problem Understanding does not require both excellent user evidence
and explicit root-cause analysis for a 5.
16.	Problem & User can score 5 without direct user evidence if the
problem framing itself is excellent.
17.	Produce reviewer-facing explanations and exceptional flags alongside
scores.
18.	Do not resolve contradictory evidence; flag it to humans.
19.	Judge is strictly closed-book.
20.	Explicitly ignore presentation quality.
21.	One strong, relevant piece of evidence can support a 5.
22.	Use holistic best-fit scoring.
23.	Force a single integer score while allowing borderline cases to be
explained.
24.	Reserve reviewer flags for exceptional/noteworthy matters.
25.	Score absolutely against the rubric, without comparison with other
teams.
26.	Make score rationales traceable to specific slide/content evidence.
27.	A rubric score of 1 maps to 2 points out of the final competition
score of 100 for each 10%-weighted criterion.

11. Remaining Design Work
The scoring model itself is substantially settled.
The next phase is implementation design, which was deliberately
deferred because the deployment environment has not yet been chosen.
When continuing in a new chat, decide where the judge will run, for
example: - ChatGPT/manual judging workflow; - OpenAI API; - automated
evaluation pipeline or application.
Then design: 1. the production system/judge prompt; 2. the input
contract for slide decks; 3. a machine-readable structured output schema
if required; 4. handling of unreadable, incomplete or malformed
submissions; 5. deterministic/calibration strategy; 6. test cases and
benchmark decks for judging consistency; 7. human-review workflow and
how reviewer flags are surfaced.
A useful next prompt in a new conversation is:
Using the Product Thinking AI Judge design in this Markdown file as
the agreed baseline, help me design the production implementation. Do
not reopen settled rubric decisions unless you identify a material
contradiction or implementation failure mode.
